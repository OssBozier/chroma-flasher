Flashing a Chroma 29 with the CC Debugger

How to take one Chroma 29 from stock firmware to OpenEPaperLink: sort it, wire it, back it up, flash it and check it. You don't need the radio parts for this, because a tag that flashed correctly shows the OEPL splash screen on its own display.

1Before you start

This guide drives the CC Debugger with cc-tool on Linux, which talks to it directly and needs no TI driver. On Windows, the debugger is passed through to WSL with usbipd-win. That also works on Windows on ARM, where TI's x86-only driver and its Flash Programmer can't run. Install steps for cc-tool (with the small patch it needs) and for these scripts are in the README.

2Connect the debugger (each session, WSL)

On Linux, a udev rule gives you access once and for all (see the README). Under WSL, bind the debugger once per USB port in an admin PowerShell (usbipd bind --busid <BUSID>; usbipd list shows it). After that, every time the debugger is plugged in, and after a reboot, it has to be attached to WSL again and given open permissions. One command does both and then checks the connection:

WSL
cc-attach

With no tag connected it prints Programmer: CC Debugger and No target detected. That's the correct result until a tag is wired up. chroma-flash runs it for you when the debugger isn't reachable.

What it does: it finds the debugger's BUSID by its USB ID (0451:16a2), runs usbipd attach --wsl, fixes the device permissions as root through wsl.exe -u root (no sudo password needed), then runs cc-tool -t.

3Sort the tags by serial number

The serial is on the label on the back: 2 letters, 8 digits and 1 letter, for example JA10012345B. The last letter decides the firmware image, and the first two letters decide whether the tag can be flashed at all. If you have a batch, sort it into piles before flashing anything.

The images are in OpenEPaperLink's binaries/Tag folder. chroma-flash picks the newest one for the serial's last letter. Always flash a _full image. The _ota files are only for over-the-air updates.

4Wire the debugger cable

The loose end of the ribbon is a 2×5 socket with 2.54 mm spacing. The male end of a jumper wire pushes straight into each hole. Five signals go to the tag. The tag's power pad also connects to debugger pin 9, which lets the debugger power the tag so the batteries can stay out.

Find pin 1

  1. At the red-stripe end there are two holes, one in each row. One is pin 1 and the other is pin 2.
  2. Look for a small triangle moulded into the socket body. On that cable it's a ▼ on the key side, at the red-stripe end, pointing at the face. Pin 1 is the hole in the row next to the key, at that end.
  3. Confirm with a multimeter if you have one: plug the debugger into USB, set the meter to DC volts, and put the probes in your pin 1 and in the hole at the far end of the same row (pin 9). You should read about 3.3 V. If you read nothing, you've picked the even row, so swap.
  4. No mark and no meter? Guess, as long as the batteries are out. If the row is wrong, pin 9's 3.3 V lands in an unused hole and the tag gets no power at all, so nothing is harmed. You'll see a red LED and No target detected. Move every wire across to the other row and try again.

Wire assignments

Use the same colours every time so a glance tells you if a wire is in the wrong hole. chroma-flash shows these colours on its status board and speaks the three probe colours when it's time to hold them. If your wires differ, set WIRE_DD, WIRE_DC, WIRE_RESET, WIRE_VDD and WIRE_GND in its config.

All five pads are on the battery side of the board, the side marked CR2450. The side with the black CC1110 chip has no test pads. The photo in step 5 shows exactly where each one is.

Make the power Y-lead (once)

Pin 2 and pin 9 both go to the tag's TP4. Pin 2 lets the debugger sense the tag's voltage, and pin 9 supplies 3.3 V to power it. A socket hole only takes one jumper, so join two jumpers into one lead:

  1. Take two jumpers with a male end (red and orange). Cut each about 10 cm from the male end and strip 5 mm.
  2. Strip one end of a third red wire, which will run to the tag. Twist all three bare ends together and solder them.
  3. Cover the joint with heat-shrink or electrical tape so it can't touch anything else.
Power from one source only. With pin 9 connected, the debugger powers the tag, so take the batteries out. Powering a tag from both the debugger and its batteries at once is one of the few ways to damage it.

5Reach the test pads

Rev B (serial ends in B)

The pads are on the battery side of the board, under the grey battery frame. They can't be reached through the battery compartment, so the board has to come out of the case.

  1. Take the batteries out.
  2. Start at a corner. Slide the plastic tool between the white front and the grey back, and pry them apart.
  3. The board sits in the grey frame with its chip side facing you. Release it from the frame's clips, starting at the end away from the display connector, and lift it out. The display stays attached by its cable.
  4. Turn the board over onto something soft so the side marked CR2450 faces up. Hinge it on the display cable like a page, without pulling or twisting it.
  5. Find the five pads using the pad map below.
  6. After flashing, seat the board back in the frame and press the two halves together until they snap.

Rev C (serial ends in C)

The test pads can be reached from the battery compartment, so the case stays closed.

  1. Open the battery door and take the batteries out.
  2. Find the test-pad labels printed next to the pads.
  3. Check them against the OpenEPaperLink wiki's Chroma 29 page before you connect anything.

For volume, a 3D-printed jig holds the pogo pins on the pads: chroma29_C_jig_v1.0.stl for rev C and Chroma29_Flashing_Jig_v1.5.stl for rev B, both in OpenEPaperLink's Hardware folder.

Rev B pad map

  • The middle row runs across the board between the two curved battery clips. In the case, it sits under the frame's bar between the two battery holes. TP4 is the end next to J2, the big solder blob, and TP2 is the other end.
  • The corner group is at the display-connector end, on the long edge that has the notch. TP8 is the corner pad. TP5 and TP3 run along the edge from it, and TP10 and TP11 sit above it.
  • TP3, TP5 and TP8 are close together, so check for solder bridges between them before powering up. Keep the iron off the battery clips, which soak up heat.
Protect the display cable. Never pry between the display glass and the bezel. The display's cable is glued to the board and can tear off if it's flexed. Keep the board flat while you work and don't let it hang by the cable.

First tag: solder, don't hold

For the very first tag, solder a short wire to each of the five pads instead of holding pogo pins against them. That takes contact problems out of the picture while you prove the process works. Cut a male-ended jumper, solder the bare end to the pad with a quick touch of the iron, and push the male end into the socket. Switch to pogo pins or a jig once one tag has flashed.

The rev B pad map comes from an EDK286 Issue 9 board. If yours doesn't match it, especially an Issue 8 or Issue 12, photograph it and check it against the OpenEPaperLink wiki before wiring.

6Connect and check the chip

  1. Batteries out. Check every wire against the table in step 4, especially that TP4 (VBAT) and TP8 (GND) aren't swapped.
  2. Plug the ribbon into the debugger, then plug the debugger into USB. Under WSL, run cc-attach.
  3. With chroma-flash, that's all: it waits for the chip and says "chip found" once it has answered three times in a row.
  4. By hand: press the Reset button on the debugger, and the LED should turn green. The debugger only looks for the chip at power-up or when Reset is pressed. Then cc-tool -t should print Target: CC1110.

If nothing answers, see Troubleshooting. Don't flash by hand until you've seen Target: CC1110.

7Flash

Hands-free, with chroma-flash

Start it, hold the label up to the camera (or type the serial), then hold the probes. It speaks each step, so you can watch the probes instead of the screen.

serial read from the label
chroma-flash
serial known
chroma-flash JA10012345B --no-backup

It finds the chip, erases, writes and verifies the image, restarts the tag, then reads the tag's MAC back and checks it against the label. A lost factory serial is patched in automatically. Each tag gets a log in the data folder. chroma-flash --help lists the options.

By hand, with cc-tool

Set the tag's serial number and the matching image first. Every command after that uses those two values.

set per tag
SN=JA10012345B     # the serial from this tag's label
IMG=OpenEPaperLink/binaries/Tag/chroma29_full_0015.bin
# for a serial ending in C use chroma29_8151_full_0013.bin instead
BK=backups
mkdir -p "$BK"
1 · confirm the chip
cc-tool -t
2 · back up the stock firmware
cc-tool -r "$BK/${SN}_stock.hex"

The backup lets you put the tag back to stock if you ever need to. Factory tags are usually read-locked, so this step fails or produces an empty file. That's fine; carry on, because the erase in the next step clears the lock.

3 · erase, write, verify
cc-tool -e -w "$IMG" -v
4 · restart the tag
cc-tool --reset

Step 3 takes a few seconds and finishes with a verify result. If a wire slips during any step, re-seat it and run that step again. Nothing is lost by retrying.

8Check the result

  1. After the reset, the display redraws with the OpenEPaperLink splash screen. E-paper refresh is slow, so give it several seconds.
  2. Read the MAC address shown on the splash screen. It should be 4467 followed by the digits of the serial.
  3. Disconnect the debugger, close the case, and note the serial, revision and result.
If the MAC ends in eight zeros the tag's stored serial number is corrupt. chroma-flash fixes this for you. By hand, write the real serial into a copy of the image with patch_sn.py from Tag_FW_Chroma (Chroma_Tag_FW/patch_sn), and flash that copy. patch_sn.py edits the file in place, so never point it at the original.
WSL · fix a lost serial
cp "$IMG" "$BK/${SN}_patched.bin"
cd Tag_FW_Chroma/Chroma_Tag_FW/patch_sn
uv run --no-project --with intelhex python patch_sn.py "$BK/${SN}_patched.bin" "$SN"
cc-tool -e -w "$BK/${SN}_patched.bin" -v && cc-tool --reset

Never give two tags the same serial. The serial becomes the tag's network address, and two tags with the same one will fight over updates.

9Troubleshooting

What you seeWhat it meansFix
CC Debugger device not foundThe debugger isn't attached to WSL.Run cc-attach (WSL), or check it's plugged in.
libusb_open failed, Access deniedNo permission on the USB device.WSL: run cc-attach (a replug resets it). Linux: add the udev rule from the README.
LED red, No target detectedThe debugger can't see the chip.Check the five wires and that pin 1 is really pin 1. Make sure TP4 has power. By hand, press the debugger's Reset button and try again.
Chip answers once with a random status (0F, 1F, 03), then notHalf contact, or the tag is losing power.Check the red (VBAT) and black (GND) leads first, then press the DD probe (TP3) firmly.
LED green, but verify failsA data wire is making poor contact.Re-seat or re-solder DC and DD, then run step 3 again.
Backup fails or the file is emptyThe stock firmware is read-locked.Expected on some tags. Carry on with step 3.
No splash screen after resetWrong image for the revision, or the display cable is disturbed.Check the serial's last letter against step 3. Flash the other image if needed. Check the display cable is still seated.
MAC ends in 00000000The stored serial is corrupt.chroma-flash patches it. By hand, patch a copy of the image (step 8) and reflash.

10What can and can't break

Safe to retry
  • A wire slipping mid-flash. Erase and flash again.
  • Flashing the wrong image. Flash the right one.
  • A corrupt serial. Fix it with patch_sn.py.
  • A locked chip. Erasing clears the lock.
The debug interface is built into the chip's hardware, so firmware can't break it.
Can cause permanent damage
  • Batteries in and debugger power connected.
  • VBAT and GND swapped, or shorted together.
  • Holding the iron on a pad until it lifts.
  • Prying at the display edge, or tearing the display cable.
  • Static: touch something grounded before handling an open board.
